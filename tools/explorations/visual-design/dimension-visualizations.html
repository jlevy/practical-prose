Visual notes & sources

Background on each candidate — where the form comes from, what it does well on this data shape (6 grouped categories × 20 ordinal axes × multiple docs), and where it breaks down. Use these to think about new visuals worth trying.

1 · Grouped Heatmap

Tabular · color saturation encoding

Lineage: Edward Tufte’s table-graphics (The Visual Display of Quantitative Information, 1983) and the conditional-formatting heatmaps that became standard in business intelligence in the 2000s (Excel, Tableau). The GitHub contribution graph (2013) cemented the small-square-saturation idiom in everyday UI.

Strengths: scales gracefully to many documents, exact values are still readable in each cell, group bands at the top tie columns to color, the layout is print-friendly and accessible to screen readers.

Tradeoffs: reads as a spreadsheet first and a picture second; you have to learn the encoding before patterns pop; with many docs the doc-name column dominates the page width.

2 · Score Strip

Sparkline row · one document per line

Lineage: Edward Tufte coined "sparklines" in Beautiful Evidence (2006) for word-sized graphics that sit inline with prose. The horizontal strip variant shows up in financial dashboards (Bloomberg, Splunk row indicators) and in github-style activity strips.

Strengths: very compact, drops cleanly into a list of files or PRs, the row is reading-order so the eye scans from left (purpose) to right (judgment) in the same order as the rubric.

Tradeoffs: tiny cells make any single dimension hard to identify without hover; absolute score is harder to read than the heatmap because the cells are smaller.

3 · Hex Honeycomb

Hex grid · row-per-group

Lineage: hex-binning maps from the cartography world (Mike Bostock’s D3 hexbin examples; Carto’s 2014 hex-tile choropleths) and the periodic-table-of-elements layout, where every cell is the same shape but labels and color carry meaning.

Strengths: the 6 rows of (4, 3, 3, 3, 4, 3) hex tiles map one-to-one to the rubric groups, the shape is decorative without being noisy, every dim gets a label, and the whole figure fits in a few inches.

Tradeoffs: hexagons are not a canonical shape for ordinal data; readers may search for a meaning in the hexagonal-ness that isn’t there.

4 · Radial Spider / Radar

Polar line graph · score = radius

Lineage: the Kiviat diagram, introduced by Kenneth Kolence and Philip Kiviat in 1973 for visualizing computer performance metrics. Popularized for human-readable use by PISA education reports, Strava / Garmin athletic profiles, and the "competency wheels" of HR / talent tooling.

Strengths: shape recognition is fast — a balanced doc is a regular polygon, a weak group is a visible dent; group-tinted arc backgrounds let the eye associate spoke clusters with the rubric groups.

Tradeoffs: the closed polygon overstates area (a doc that scores 5,1,5,1 looks much worse than 3,3,3,3 even though both mean 3); axis order changes the perceived shape; comparing two polygons overlaid is visually noisy past two documents.

5 · Coxcomb / Nightingale Rose

Polar area chart · score = √(petal radius)

Lineage: Florence Nightingale’s 1858 "Diagram of the Causes of Mortality in the Army in the East," drawn to persuade Parliament that sanitation, not battle, was killing soldiers in Crimea. Modern uses: BBC and FT election graphics, Stefanie Posavec’s data portraits, Giorgia Lupi’s Dear Data (2016).

Strengths: more visually punchy than radar — soft spots really stick out as short petals; works well as a single, "iconic" chart for a one-document report header; the √-scaling keeps low scores legible without exaggerating high ones.

Tradeoffs: area encoding is harder to read precisely than length; the form is less familiar than bars or radar so readers need a moment to learn it; like radar, hard to overlay multiple docs.

6 · Sunburst

Radial treemap · 2 rings, hierarchy + value

Lineage: John Stasko and Eugene Zhang introduced the sunburst at IEEE InfoVis 2000 as a radial successor to the treemap. Popularized by Mike Bostock’s D3 sunburst examples and by disk-inventory tools (Disk Inventory X, WinDirStat, Daisy Disk).

Strengths: a single figure carries both the group structure and the per-dim scores — inner ring for groups, outer ring saturation for scores; the centred mean gives a one-glance overall verdict.

Tradeoffs: outer-ring arc lengths are equal here (each dim gets the same wedge), so the chart only encodes value with color — meaning we’re carrying the sunburst’s decorative cost without using its strongest dimension; not as efficient as the heatmap for actual reading.

7 · Group Small Multiples

Trellis cards · one card per group, bars across docs

Lineage: trellis / lattice plots from William Cleveland and Robert McGill at Bell Labs (1980s), formalized in Cleveland’s Visualizing Data (1993); Tufte’s "small multiples" principle (Envisioning Information, 1990); the ggplot2 facet_wrap idiom that made the pattern standard in modern data analysis.

Strengths: the best layout for "I want to look inside one group across several drafts" — the eye walks one card at a time without losing context; the per-dim mean column on the right is a built-in roll-up.

Tradeoffs: no single-figure summary; takes the most vertical space of any candidate; comparing across groups means jumping between cards rather than scanning a line.

8 · Bar Cards (overlay-text)

One card per doc · text on top of the bar

Lineage: the in-cell data-bar idiom from Excel 2007 and Stephen Few’s bullet charts (Information Dashboard Design, 2006), fused with the progress-bar / Likert row used in modern product UI (Linear, GitHub Insights, Notion). The two-copy text-clip trick for legibility against a moving fill is the same technique that powers e.g. Apple Music’s scrubber-time readout.

Strengths: the most space-efficient per-doc view — bar and label share the same row, no separate name column. Dim labels read at sentence-length speed (lowercase sans-serif), group sections carry the design-system MDI icons (compass-rose, quill, anchor, ruler, scale-balance) so groups identify themselves visually as well as by color. NA / ERR get full-width muted bars so they can never be misread as low scores.

Tradeoffs: only sensibly comparable side-by-side (one card per doc); for many docs the page gets horizontally crowded. Two-copy text labels mean the dim name is drawn twice per row — cheap, but worth noting if you ever wanted to animate the fill.

9 · Bidirectional Bars

10 left + 10 right · per-dim hue · central numeric spine

Lineage: the back-to-back / opposed bar chart used in demographic population pyramids (US Census, Statistics Canada) and in Hans Rosling–style gender / age comparisons, combined with the rubric’s grouped structure. The per-dim hue variation (slight hue rotation within each group family) is the design pattern Datawrapper and the FT use for grouped categorical palettes — colors that read as "the same family" without being monochromatic.

Strengths: reads as a single "shape" per document — the silhouette formed by the bar lengths across all 20 dims is glanceable; the prominent central numbers give the precise scores while the bars give the visual; group identity is doubly encoded (hue family + section header with MDI icon); Reasoning-on-top pairs its 4 dims row-for-row with Purpose’s 4, giving a strong top-section anchor.

Tradeoffs: the right side has one more group transition (Reasoning → Grounding → Judgment) than the left (Purpose → Expression), so the bottom rows can drift slightly out of alignment; the dim text is on the colored fill only, so very low scores can truncate the name on a narrow bar (the leading dim id "P1." mitigates).

Design system & palette

HSL tokens · group hues + valence score ramp

All color tokens on this page are defined with hsl() and aligned to tools/docs/design/design-system.md, the project’s color policy of record. Two axes are kept orthogonal: group identity (one hue and one saturation per group: Purpose 72, Expression 206, Form 30, Grounding 162, Reasoning 329, Judgment 278; lightness alone shifts between light/dark and between surface/ink/dim roles) and score value (a five-stop red→green valence ramp from hsl(0 70% 35%) at 1 to hsl(140 60% 20%) at 5, plus a muted gray for NA and a faint red wash for ERR).

Each visual decides which axis to encode in color. The tabular visuals (heatmap, strip, hex, sunburst outer) use the score ramp in cells and rely on adjacent bands / labels for group identity. The polar visuals (radar, coxcomb, group cards) keep the group hue on the mark and let length / radius / area carry the score. The cream-paper reading surface is local to this report; it can be swapped for the design system’s default surface tokens when the page moves into the renderer.

Other visual languages worth a look

Not built here · candidates for a second pass

  • Parallel coordinates (Alfred Inselberg, 1985) — one vertical axis per dimension, each doc is a polyline. Strong for spotting which docs cluster together across many axes; weak when the axes are grouped and the eye wants to read groups.
  • Bullet charts (Stephen Few, Information Dashboard Design, 2006) — a per-dim horizontal bar with target and qualitative bands. Could replace the heatmap cell with a tiny bullet for richer encoding (score + NA / ERR + the 1-5 anchor bands).
  • Marimekko / mosaic — stacked columns sized by group weight. Useful if some groups carry more "weight" than others in a weighted rollup; not useful with equal weighting.
  • Slope graph (Tufte, Visual Display, 1983) — pairs of axes with lines between, ideal for before / after comparisons of the same doc across two drafts. Worth keeping in mind for the pprose compare report.
  • Chord / arc diagram — for the cross-dimension cascades (Suitability → Relevance, Verifiability → Factuality, Soundness → Parsimony). The current rubric encodes those as a table; a small chord diagram could make the prereq relationships visible.
  • Annotated text mark-up — not a chart per se, but a "scored doc preview" with inline dim markers and color-coded margins, in the spirit of The Pudding’s long-form essays. Could be the most useful representation for a single doc.
