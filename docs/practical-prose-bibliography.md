# Practical Prose Bibliography

Version: v0.1 (last update 2026-05-11)\
Joshua Levy (github.com/jlevy), assembled with agent assistance

While not exhaustive, this curated bibliography of notable past and present works on
practical writing covers many notable works in English.

Within each section, entries are ordered roughly by publication date.
Entries carry a bibliographic note with author, title, publisher, year, and a stable URL
where one is available; book and journal entries follow Chicago Manual of Style
formatting, while standards, web essays, and community resources use a lighter web
citation since a strict Chicago form would obscure rather than clarify the reference.

## Major Traditions in Practical Writing

Practical writing draws from several traditions: plain language for economy, science for
checkability, journalism for verification and proportion, technical communication for
usability and maintenance, and literary craft for attention to the reader’s experience.

The table below maps those traditions to principles they support.
The bibliography entries that follow provide source-level detail.

| Tradition | Contribution | Closest principles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plain language and style reform** | Economy, directness, concrete language, respect for reader time | Essential; Lucid; Humane |
| **Reader cognition and audience modeling** | Sentence flow, reader expectations, skim behavior, context calibration | Purposeful; Lucid; Humane |
| **Functional writing and rhetoric** | Reader value, argument structure, useful persuasion, stated purpose | Purposeful; Truthful; Humane |
| **Science and epistemic calibration** | Checkability, uncertainty, falsification, reproducible reasoning | Truthful; Verifiable |
| **Journalism and reporting** | Verification, proportion, source discipline, fairness | Truthful; Verifiable; Humane |
| **Structure, genre, and information design** | Form matched to task, visual integrity, navigable organization | Purposeful; Essential; Lucid |
| **Content design and UX writing** | Audience-tested content, scannability, task-oriented prose | Purposeful; Lucid; Humane |
| **Accessibility and inclusive design** | Standards-based access for people with disabilities, plain-language for varying abilities, equivalent experience across modalities | Lucid; Humane; Maintainable |
| **Technical communication and docs as code** | Usability, maintainability, ownership, versioned documentation | Maintainable; Purposeful |
| **Scientific reporting and editorial standards** | Reproducible methods, source discipline, citation rigor, structured reporting | Truthful; Verifiable; Maintainable |
| **Legal and regulatory writing** | Precision under adversarial reading, binding language, stratified audiences, citation discipline | Truthful; Verifiable; Maintainable; Lucid |

## Scope and Limits

This bibliography is strongest for English-language practical prose, technical
documentation, plain-language reform, scientific writing, journalism, content design,
information architecture, and software-adjacent documentation.
It should be supplemented when working in:

- **Localization and translation** of technical content.
- **Legal and regulatory mandates** beyond the US Plain Writing Act and ISO 24495 family
  (jurisdiction-specific accessibility, consumer-protection, or product-information
  rules).
- **Accessibility compliance** for specific audiences (WCAG, EAA, Section 508), as the
  works cited here treat accessibility as one input into clarity rather than as the
  authoritative standard.
- **Discipline-specific academic writing** conventions (legal scholarship, biomedical
  reporting beyond IMRaD, etc.).
- **Non-English rhetorical traditions** (the bibliography is anchored in Anglophone
  rhetoric and English-language style reform).

## Essential Modern Works

A few works have disproportionately shaped modern thinking on modern, practical English
writing. We list these up front for those looking for a shortlist.
(Each appears again in its thematic section below with full citation.)

1. **Orwell, “Politics and the English Language”** (1946). The founding modern
   plain-language essay.
2. **Strunk & White, *The Elements of Style*** (1959). The most-quoted American style
   manual.
3. **Williams, *Style: Toward Clarity and Grace*** (1990). The most rigorous account of
   sentence-level clarity.
4. **Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*** (1983). Foundational for
   the visual side of practical communication.
5. **Pinker, *The Sense of Style*** (2014). The modern synthesis with cognitive science.
6. **McEnerney, “The Craft of Writing Effectively”** (2014, YouTube).
   The functional-value position in a 90-minute lecture.

## Classical Foundations

- **Aristotle, *Rhetoric*** (4th c. BCE)[^aristotle-rhetoric]. The foundational
  treatment of persuasion.
  Introduces the three modes of proof (ethos, pathos, logos) and the three genres of
  public speech. The conceptual ancestor of every later book on functional writing.

## Plain Language and Style Reform

- **Twain, “Fenimore Cooper’s Literary Offenses”** (1895)[^twain-cooper]. Caustic
  critique of bad prose framed as nineteen “rules governing literary art” that Cooper
  allegedly violated. The comic precursor to plain-language manifestos.
- **Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*** (1926)[^fowler-meu]. The
  foundational English usage guide.
  Opinionated and witty; later editions are progressively more descriptive.
- **Orwell, “Politics and the English Language”** (1946)[^orwell-politics]. Argues that
  vague, pretentious language enables sloppy, dishonest thinking, and that honest
  thinking requires plain prose as a discipline.
  Closes with six rules of style.
- **Gowers, *Plain Words*** (1948)[^gowers-plain-words]. Commissioned by HM Treasury to
  improve the prose of British civil servants.
  The defining mid-century British plain-English manifesto.
- **Lucas, *Style*** (1955)[^lucas-style]. Learned, opinionated meditation by a
  Cambridge classicist.
  Style as character: brevity, courtesy, lucidity, and good manners toward the reader
  are ethics, not decoration.
- **Strunk & White, *The Elements of Style*** (1959)[^strunk-white]. The most-quoted
  American style manual.
  Key rules: “Omit needless words” (Rule 17 in White-revised editions) and “Use
  definite, specific, concrete language” (Rule 16). Strunk’s 1918 lecture notes, revised
  by White for the 1959 Macmillan edition.
- **Barzun, *Simple and Direct*** (1975)[^barzun-simple-and-direct]. Organized by
  principles rather than rules; writing as a craft of thought-on-the-page.
- **Zinsser, *On Writing Well*** (1976)[^zinsser]. The most-recommended modern
  nonfiction writing book.
  Famous Ch. 2 ("Simplicity") advice: “strip every sentence to its cleanest components.”
  Zinsser’s four articles of faith: clarity, simplicity, brevity, humanity.
- **Garner, *Garner’s Modern English Usage*** (1998, current 5th ed.
  2022)[^garner-meu]. The dominant contemporary American usage authority; the modern
  successor to Fowler.
  (Garner’s legal-writing works are cited under *Legal and Regulatory Writing*.)
- **Clark, *Writing Tools*** (2006)[^clark-writing-tools]. Fifty short chapters on
  writing technique from the Poynter Institute.
  Accessible and modern.
- **US Plain Writing Act of 2010**[^plain-writing-act]. Requires federal executive
  agencies to use plain writing in public-facing documents.
- **ASD-STE100, *Simplified Technical English*** (current Issue 9, 2024)[^asd-ste100].
  The controlled-language specification originally developed for aerospace maintenance
  documentation by the AeroSpace and Defence Industries Association of Europe.
  Constrains vocabulary, grammar, and sentence structure to a defined subset to improve
  translation, comprehension, and safety in technical content; broadly applicable beyond
  aerospace.
- **Klinkenborg, *Several Short Sentences About Writing*** (2012)[^klinkenborg].
  Aphorisms and observations; itself an argument for the short sentence.
- **ISO 24495-1:2023, *Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and
  guidelines***[^iso-24495-1]. The current international standard for plain language.
  Establishes principles and guidelines for developing plain-language documents; applies
  beyond public-facing communication to technical writing and controlled languages.
  Part 2 (ISO 24495-2:2025) covers legal writing; subsequent parts of the ISO 24495
  family are in development or publication workflow as of 2026: Part 3 (science
  writing), Part 4 (organizational implementation, ISO/DIS), and Part 5 (information
  design, ISO/WD). Consult the ISO catalogue for current status.

## Reader Cognition and Sentence Craft

- **Lanham, *Revising Prose* and *Analyzing Prose*** (1979, 1983)[^lanham]. Introduced
  the “Paramedic Method” for revision: circle prepositions, find the action, find who is
  kicking whom. Frames writing as structurally lean.
- **Williams, *Style: Toward Clarity and Grace*** (1990)[^williams-style]. The most
  rigorous account of sentence-level clarity.
  Two foundational moves: characters-as-subjects + actions-as-verbs (avoid
  nominalization), and old-information-before-new.
- **Williams & Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*** (1981, current 12th ed.
  2017)[^williams-bizup-lessons]. The textbook companion to Williams’s trade *Style*.
  Same principles, more exercises.
- **Gopen & Swan, “The Science of Scientific Writing”** (1990)[^gopen-swan]. Introduces
  *topic position* and *stress position* as the load-bearing constraints on sentence
  structure.
- **Thomas & Turner, *Clear and Simple as the Truth*** (1994)[^thomas-turner].
  Introduces *classic style*: prose as a window onto a world both writer and reader can
  see. The conceptual ancestor of Pinker’s *Sense of Style*.
- **Pinker, *The Sense of Style*** (2014)[^pinker]. The modern synthesis.
  Integrates Thomas & Turner’s classic style with cognitive science (the curse of
  knowledge) into a single accessible style guide.

## Functional Value and Audience

- **Adler & Van Doren, *How to Read a Book*** (1940; rev.
  1972)[^adler-van-doren]. The reader-side complement to Strunk & White.
  Four levels of reading (elementary, inspectional, analytical, syntopical) describe
  what a practical writer should make easy.
- **Trimble, *Writing with Style*** (1975, 3rd ed.
  2010)[^trimble]. “conversational” style guide; emphasizes empathy and courtesy toward
  the reader.
- **Becker, *Writing for Social Scientists*** (1986)[^becker]. The defining book on
  academic writing as a process.
  Procedural advice (cut the first paragraph; write the way you talk) has shaped
  social-science methods training.
- **McEnerney, “The Craft of Writing Effectively”** (2014, YouTube)[^mcenerney].
  90-minute public lecture, widely circulated as the clearest statement of the
  functional-value position: writing is not the transmission of ideas; it is the
  creation of value for a community of readers.
- **Graham, “How to Write Usefully”** (2020)[^graham-useful]. Defines useful writing as
  importance + novelty + correctness + strength.

## Structure, Genre, and Craft

- **Sloane, *The Craft of Writing*** (1979)[^sloane]. Distilled lectures from the
  founder of the Bread Loaf Writers’ Conference.
  An opening promises a contract to the reader; structure either delivers or breaks it.
- **McPhee, *Draft No.
  4*** (2017)[^mcphee]. Collected essays from *The New Yorker* on McPhee’s craft.
  The central piece, “Structure” (2013), diagrams the underlying shapes of his pieces.
- **The journalism inverted pyramid**[^inverted-pyramid]. Late-19th-century convention,
  commonly traced to telegraph-era wire services.
- **IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion)**[^imrad]. Sollaci &
  Pereira’s fifty-year history is the authoritative source.
- **Diátaxis documentation framework** (Daniele Procida)[^diataxis]. Four-quadrant
  model: tutorials, how-to guides, technical reference, explanation.

## Visual and Information Design

- **Schriver, *Dynamics in Document Design*** (1997)[^schriver]. The foundational modern
  work on document design as a research-based discipline.
  Treats prose and graphic design as integrated; introduces empirical methods for
  evaluating whether a document’s design actually helps the reader.
  Widely cited in technical communication and content design.
- **Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*** (1983)
  [^tufte-visual-display]. The foundational modern work on data graphics.
  Introduces the data-ink ratio, chartjunk, small multiples, and the principles of
  graphical integrity.
- **Tufte’s sequels: *Envisioning Information* (1990), *Visual Explanations* (1997),
  *Beautiful Evidence* (2006)**[^tufte-later]. Three sequels treating layering and
  separation, causation in pictures, and the critique of PowerPoint.
- **Few, *Show Me the Numbers*** (2004, 2nd ed.
  2012)[^few-show-me]. Practical applied counterpart to Tufte; covers table design as
  rigorously as graph design.
- **Cairo, *The Functional Art* (2012) and *How Charts Lie* (2019)**[^cairo]. Modern,
  journalism-informed treatments of information graphics.
  The design textbook and the reader-side complement on visual literacy.

## Content Design and Usability

- **Nielsen Norman Group, “Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the
  Web”** (1997)[^nngroup-scannable]. Foundational web-writing research.
  Argues that web readers scan first, read second; recommends concise, scannable,
  objective prose. Maps directly to the “skim recoverability” rule in P1 Suitability and
  to F1 Organization in `practical-prose-guidelines.md`.
- **Nielsen Norman Group ongoing research**[^nngroup-ongoing]. Continually updated body
  of usability research and writing guidance; UX-focused articles on scannable content,
  plain language for the web, accessibility, and information architecture.
- **Krug, *Don’t Make Me Think*** (2000, current 3rd ed.
  2014)[^krug-dont-make-me-think]. The standard short book on web usability.
  Treats clarity, conventions, and minimal cognitive load as the central design goals;
  pairs cleanly with NN/g research and content design practice.
- **Redish, *Letting Go of the Words: Writing Web Content That Works*** (2007, current
  2nd ed. 2012)[^redish]. The defining book on web content design from a usability
  perspective. Frames web writing as task-oriented prose audited by user testing, not
  stylistic decoration.
- **Halvorson & Rach, *Content Strategy for the Web*** (2nd ed., 2012)[^halvorson-rach].
  The standard introduction to content strategy: aligning what content gets written, by
  whom, on what cadence, with the goals of the organization and the needs of the reader.
- **Richards, *Content Design*** (2017)[^richards-content-design]. Named the discipline.
  Content design starts from user needs and tasks, evaluates content empirically, and
  treats writing as one component of usability.
  Developed inside the UK Government Digital Service.
- **Podmajersky, *Strategic Writing for UX*** (2019)[^podmajersky]. UX-writing manual
  focused on conversation patterns, microcopy, and editorial governance for product
  interfaces.
- **Digital.gov, *Plain language guide series***[^digital-gov-plain]. US federal
  content-design and audience-testing guidance.
  Frames plain language as not only writing but also designing and testing content for
  audience understanding: a usability discipline, not a style choice.

## Accessibility and Inclusive Design

Accessibility is not a stylistic afterthought; it is a precondition for many readers.
Practical prose that ignores accessibility excludes part of its audience by default.
The standards below are normative for public-sector, regulated, and most enterprise
documentation.

- **W3C WCAG 2.2, *Web Content Accessibility Guidelines*** (2023)[^wcag22]. The
  international consensus standard for accessible web content.
  Four principles (perceivable, operable, understandable, robust); three conformance
  levels (A, AA, AAA). The W3C recommends use of the latest WCAG version, and most
  jurisdictions reference WCAG 2.1 AA or higher as their baseline.
- **W3C Web Accessibility Initiative (WAI) supplementary guidance**[^w3c-wai]. Companion
  W3C resources beyond the normative WCAG itself: techniques, ARIA authoring practices,
  evaluation methodology, and accessibility statement generators.
- **EN 301 549, *Accessibility requirements for ICT products and services*** (current
  V3.2.1, 2021)[^en-301-549]. The European harmonized standard for procurement and
  conformance under the EU Web Accessibility Directive and the European Accessibility
  Act. References WCAG with additional ICT-specific requirements.
- **US Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act** (1973, as amended)[^section-508]. The US
  federal accessibility statute and the basis for the Section 508 Refresh (2018), which
  harmonized US federal accessibility requirements with WCAG 2.0 AA and EN 301 549.
- **WebAIM**[^webaim]. Long-running practical-resource site from Utah State University’s
  CPD: tutorials, checklists, the WAVE evaluation tool, and the *WebAIM Million* report
  on the accessibility of the top one million home pages.
  The first stop for practitioners who need an applied (rather than normative)
  reference.

## Information Architecture and Structured Authoring

For agent-readable and agent-authored documents, structure is a retrieval and parsing
affordance, not just editorial polish.
Content models, schemas, and stable identifiers let downstream agents (and humans)
navigate, link, and update reliably.

- **Rockley & Cooper, *Managing Enterprise Content: A Unified Content Strategy*** (2nd
  ed., 2012)[^rockley-cooper]. The standard work on unified content strategy: content
  modeling, reuse, metadata, single-sourcing, and the lifecycle of structured
  documentation.
- **Hackos, *Information Development: Managing Your Documentation Projects, Portfolio,
  and People*** (2007) and earlier *Managing Your Documentation Projects* (1994) and
  *Content Management for Dynamic Web Delivery* (2002)[^hackos]. The dominant US voice
  in technical-communication leadership: planning, audience analysis, information
  modeling, and the lifecycle of documentation as a project rather than as one-off
  prose.
- **OASIS DITA (Darwin Information Typing Architecture)**[^dita]. The dominant
  structured-authoring standard for technical content.
  Topic-based authoring with typed information units (concept, task, reference),
  enabling reliable reuse and transformation.
  DITA 1.3 remains the stable OASIS standard; DITA 2.0 specification work and toolchain
  support (DITA-OT) are emerging as of 2026.
- **Diátaxis Documentation Framework** (Daniele Procida)[^diataxis]. Already cited under
  Structure, Genre, and Craft; relevant here as a content-model framework for
  agent-readable docs.

## Academic and Scientific Writing

- **Booth, Colomb, Williams et al., *The Craft of Research*** (1995, current 5th ed.
  2024)[^booth-craft-of-research]. The standard academic-research methods book.
  Most-assigned text on academic writing in US graduate programs.
- **Sword, *Stylish Academic Writing*** (2012)[^sword-stylish]. Study of stylish
  academic prose drawn from a corpus of 1,000 articles.
  Argument: jargon and stiffness are choices, not requirements of academic genre.
- **Hayot, *The Elements of Academic Style*** (2014)[^hayot]. Humanities-oriented
  complement to Williams.
  The “uneven U” paragraph model is widely taught.
- **NASEM, *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science*** (2019)
  [^nasem-reproducibility]. The most recent consensus statement on scientific reporting
  norms.
- ***The Chicago Manual of Style*** (1906, current 18th ed.
  2024)[^chicago-manual]. The dominant US humanities and trade-publishing style guide.
  The 18th edition adds explicit guidance on diversity, accessibility, inclusive
  language, and use of AI tools alongside the traditional citation, mechanics, and
  manuscript-preparation content.
- **EQUATOR Network**[^equator]. The umbrella initiative ("Enhancing the QUAlity and
  Transparency Of health Research") cataloguing reporting guidelines for the health
  sciences. Maintains a searchable library of 600+ guidelines (CONSORT, PRISMA, STROBE,
  ARRIVE, CARE, SPIRIT, and others) and pairs each with checklists, flow diagrams, and
  examples.
- **CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials)**[^consort]. The dominant
  reporting standard for randomized controlled trials.
  Mandated by most major medical journals; the checklist defines what a trial report
  must contain to be evaluable.
- **PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses)**
  (current PRISMA 2020)[^prisma]. The dominant standard for systematic reviews and
  meta-analyses; the four-phase flow diagram is a structural fixture of the genre.
- **ICMJE, *Recommendations for the Conduct, Reporting, Editing, and Publication of
  Scholarly Work in Medical Journals***[^icmje]. The International Committee of Medical
  Journal Editors’ standing recommendations on authorship, conflicts of interest,
  manuscript preparation, and editorial responsibilities; adopted by ~5,000 journals.
- **APA Publication Manual** (current 7th ed., 2019)[^apa]. The dominant style guide for
  psychology and the social sciences; the source of APA citation style.
- **AMA Manual of Style** (current 11th ed., 2020)[^ama]. The dominant style guide for
  biomedical writing and the source of AMA citation style; pairs with the ICMJE
  recommendations on editorial substance.

## Epistemic Calibration and Reasoning

- **Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*** (1958)[^toulmin-uses-of-argument]. Introduces the
  Toulmin model: claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal.
  The practical framework for surfacing hidden assumptions.
- **Popper, *Conjectures and Refutations*** (1963)[^popper]. Defines falsifiability as
  the demarcation criterion between science and non-science.
  The conceptual basis for “what would refute this?”
- **Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*** (2011)[^kahneman]. Synthesis on cognitive
  bias, heuristics, and judgment under uncertainty.
  Direct relevance to the curse of knowledge and disciplined inference.
- **Tetlock & Gardner, *Superforecasting*** (2015)[^tetlock-gardner]. Calibration as a
  measurable practice.
  Pre-event committed priors, base-rate anchoring, coherent probabilities.

## Technical and Software Documentation

- **Knuth, “Literate Programming”** (1984)[^knuth-literate-programming]. Literate
  programming as a system where documentation and code share a single source (the WEB
  system).
- **Parnas & Clements, “A Rational Design Process”** (1986)[^parnas-clements].
  Documentation as a formal design artifact, treated with the same rigor as code.
- **Carroll, *The Nurnberg Funnel: Designing Minimalist Instruction for Practical
  Computer Skill*** (MIT Press, 1990) and *Minimalism Beyond the Nurnberg Funnel* (MIT
  Press, 1998)[^carroll-minimalism]. The founding research for *minimalist instruction*:
  cut overview and decoration, get the reader to a meaningful task quickly, let
  realistic projects drive the documentation.
  Continues to shape the design of quickstarts, getting-started guides, and tutorial
  tracks across the technical-communication discipline.
- **Gentle, *Docs Like Code*** (2017)[^docs-like-code]. The defining book on the
  docs-as-code movement.
- **Write the Docs community** (2013–)[^write-the-docs]. Community guide defining
  docs-as-code conventions.
- **Read the Docs**[^read-the-docs]. Representative hosting platform for
  Sphinx/MkDocs/Jupyter Book docs, with PR previews and versioned builds.
- **Microsoft Writing Style Guide**[^microsoft-style]. Online successor to the print
  *Microsoft Manual of Style* (4th ed., 2012). One of two dominant industry style guides
  for technical documentation.
  Foregrounds bias-free communication, global audience, and accessibility as first-class
  style concerns, not afterthoughts.
- **Google Developer Documentation Style Guide**[^google-style]. Google’s
  developer-documentation conventions.
  The other dominant industry style guide.
  Includes dedicated guidance on accessibility, writing for a global audience, and
  inclusive language; explicitly says project-specific style overrides the general guide
  and that writers may break a rule when doing so improves the content.
- **ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514:2022, *Systems and software engineering — Design and development
  of information for users***[^iso-26514]. International standard for the design and
  development of user information for software products.
  Successor to IEEE Std 1063; aligns with the IEC/IEEE 82079 family.
- **IEC/IEEE 82079-1:2019, *Preparation of information for use (instructions for use) of
  products — Part 1: Principles and general requirements***[^iec-82079-1]. The general
  international standard for instructions for use across product categories.
  Often paired with 26514 for software-specific elaboration.
- **ISO 24183:2024, *Technical communication — Vocabulary***[^iso-24183]. Defines the
  shared vocabulary used across technical-communication standards (audience analysis,
  information types, content models, deliverables).
  Useful when a domain demands precise terminology.

## Legal and Regulatory Writing

Legal and regulatory writing has constraints that the other practical-writing traditions
don’t fully share: adversarial reading (the prose is parsed by a reader looking for a
loophole), binding language with legal effect, stratified audiences (judge, opposing
counsel, client, regulator, public), and citation standards that are themselves a
separate craft. Modern plain-language reform emerged in significant part from
legal-writing reform, and the two remain deeply intertwined; the works below are the
legal-domain anchors specifically.

- **Richard C. Wydick, *Plain English for Lawyers*** (1979, current 6th ed.
  2019)[^wydick]. The foundational US law-school text on plain-English legal writing.
  Six concise principles (omit surplus words, use base verbs, prefer active voice, use
  familiar words, avoid noun strings, place modifiers carefully) have shaped a
  generation of legal-writing pedagogy.
- **Clarity International**[^clarity-international]. The international association of
  legal-plain-language practitioners (founded 1983 from work by Mark Adler and others).
  Publishes the *Clarity* journal; convenes international conferences on legal plain
  language.
- **US SEC Office of Investor Education and Assistance, *A Plain English Handbook: How
  to Create Clear SEC Disclosure Documents*** (1998)[^sec-plain-english]. The practical
  handbook the SEC issued to drafters of disclosure documents after the Plain English
  rule for prospectuses.
  Foreword by Warren Buffett.
  Widely influential in US regulatory and legal plain-language work.
- **Bryan A. Garner’s legal-writing reference set**[^garner-legal-set]. *Legal Writing
  in Plain English* (2001, current 3rd ed.
  2023) is the standard short manual for litigators and contract drafters; *The Winning
  Brief* (1999, current 3rd ed.
  2014) covers advocacy writing for litigators with 100 tips on brief structure,
  argument staging, and citation style; *Garner’s Dictionary of Legal Usage* (3rd ed.,
  2011\) is the legal-writing companion to *Garner’s Modern English Usage* and the
  dominant US legal-usage authority.
  Together they form the most-used contemporary US legal-writing reference set.
- **Joseph Kimble, *Lifting the Fog of Legalese: Essays on Plain Language*** (2006) and
  *Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please* (2012)[^kimble]. The dominant US scholarly
  advocate for legal plain-language reform; assembles empirical evidence that
  plain-language drafting reduces cost and improves comprehension across regulatory,
  judicial, and contractual domains.
- **plainlanguage.gov / *Federal Plain Language Guidelines*** (current edition,
  2011)[^plainlanguage-gov]. US federal plain-language guidance produced under the Plain
  Writing Act of 2010 by the Plain Language Action and Information Network (PLAIN).
  Concrete drafting advice for agency documents.
- **US Office of the Federal Register, *Document Drafting Handbook***[^ofr-ddh]. The
  federal regulatory drafting standard, governing how rules, notices, and other
  documents are written for publication in the Federal Register.
  The closest thing the US has to a regulatory-prose style guide.
- **The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation** (current 21st ed., 2020)[^bluebook].
  The dominant US legal citation standard, governing how legal sources are cited in
  scholarship, briefs, and judicial opinions.
  Produced jointly by the Columbia, Harvard, University of Pennsylvania, and Yale law
  reviews. In active use since 1926.
- **OSCOLA (Oxford Standard for the Citation of Legal Authorities)** (current 4th ed.,
  2012)[^oscola]. The UK and Commonwealth counterpart to the Bluebook; the dominant
  legal citation standard outside the US. Maintained by the Oxford Law Faculty.

## Journalism and Reporting

- **Kovach & Rosenstiel, *The Elements of Journalism*** (2001, 4th ed.
  2021)[^kovach-rosenstiel]. The standard statement of journalism’s principles.
  Verification as the central discipline; widely assigned in J-school.

## Critique of AI-Authored Prose

Work on the register, vocabulary, and rhetorical fingerprints of LLM-generated prose,
and the editorial response.
[ai-prose-corrections.md](ai-prose-corrections.md) is the binding extension that turns
this body of work into a checkable list.

**Empirical and linguistic studies.**

- **Liang et al., “Mapping the Increasing Use of LLMs in Scientific Papers”** (2024)
  [^liang-llm-mapping]. The Stanford study that established the marker-word methodology
  now widely adopted: models scientific corpora as a mixture of human and LLM text and
  tracks the post-ChatGPT shift in word frequencies.
- **Kobak et al., “Delving into LLM-Assisted Writing in Biomedical Publications through
  Excess Vocabulary”** (2025)[^kobak-delving]. Population-scale evidence for the LLM
  “fingerprint” across millions of PubMed abstracts.
  Identifies excess words (*delve*, *showcasing*, *underscores*, *pivotal*) appearing in
  ~10–30% of 2024 abstracts using a COVID-style excess-frequency method.
- **Juzek & Ward, “Why Does ChatGPT ‘Delve’ So Much?
  Exploring the Sources of Lexical Overrepresentation in Large Language Models”**
  (2024)[^juzek-ward-delve]. Tests and rules out architecture, algorithm, and
  training-data explanations, locating the cause of LLM lexical tells in RLHF feedback
  rather than the training corpus.

**Style criticism and conceptual frames.**

- **Chiang, “ChatGPT Is a Blurry JPEG of the Web”** (2023)[^chiang-blurry-jpeg]. The
  lossy-compression metaphor that most subsequent style critiques borrow: LLM prose
  reads as competent-but-blurred paraphrase rather than original thought.
- **Hoel, “The Banality of ChatGPT”** (2022)[^hoel-banality]. A practicing-writer
  critique of LLM register as statistically average prose; the durable essayistic
  counterpart to the later tweet-thread taxonomies of tells.
- **Bajohr, “Artificial and Post-Artificial Texts: On Machine Learning and the Reader’s
  Expectations of Literary and Non-Literary Writing”** (2024) [^bajohr-post-artificial].
  A literary-theoretic account of how LLM prose reshapes the reader’s default assumption
  of human authorship, distinguishing artificial style from a coming “post-artificial”
  style that performs humanness.

**Editor and writing-craft guidance.**

- **Sword, “Writing Critically with AI”** (2024)[^sword-writing-critically-with-ai].
  Extends *Stylish Academic Writing* with concrete avoidance: run AI drafts through the
  Writer’s Diet diagnostic for nominalizations (“zombie nouns”), prepositional pile-ups,
  and abstract-noun bloat, the exact bloat AI register defaults to.
- **Garner, “Prompting Prose: When Used Correctly, AI in Legal Writing Can Be a Useful
  Tool”** (2024) and *LawProse Lesson #458*[^garner-prompting-prose]. Garner’s rule: use
  AI as “amplifier, not author.”
  Specifically warns against accepting LLM cadence in argumentative prose because it
  flattens the rhetorical compression *Garner’s Modern English Usage* prizes.
- **Michel, “Criticism in the Age of AI”** (*Counter Craft*, 2025)[^michel-criticism].
  Argues for “very visible prose”: sentences that bear individual marks rather than the
  “generic averaging” LLMs produce.
  Concrete test: if a passage could be reproduced by a prompt, rewrite it.
- **Clark, Q&A in *Poynter*** (2025)[^clark-poynter-qa]. Concrete craft test against AI
  register: read the draft aloud, then have a trusted reader read it back; if the voice
  does not sound like a recognizable person who is “a little better” than the writer’s
  everyday speech, it has slid into machine-default tone.
- **Folta, “Here’s a Handy Guide to Help You Spot AI Writing”** (*Literary Hub*,
  2025)[^folta-handy-guide]. Editor-facing checklist of register tells (formulaic
  transitions, hedged universals, reflexive both-sides framing) with a rewrite
  heuristic: when you find one, replace it with a sentence containing a verifiable
  detail only the author could know.

**Practitioner rule sets and banned-phrase lists.**

Working rule sheets published by practitioners.
Quality varies; entries are publicly hosted, durably addressable, and present an
enumerated rule set rather than a complaint.

- **Lehmann, “Voice DNA”** (X/Twitter thread, 2026)[^lehmann-voice-dna]. Compact rule
  sheet circulating widely among writers using LLMs.
  Names the fatal self-negating parallel construction (“This isn’t X. This is Y.”) as
  the highest-priority deletion; lists ~50 banned phrases across dead AI language, dead
  transitions, engagement bait, AI cringe, and insider-claim framings.
- **Pandya, “stop-slop: a skill file for removing AI tells from prose”** (2025)
  [^pandya-stop-slop]. Packaged as a Claude-agent skill, combining a phrases.md banlist
  (~60–80 entries across 8 categories) with a 5-dimension 1-10 prose rescoring rubric
  for post-revision evaluation.
  The only entry that pairs a banlist with a self-grading rubric.
- **Cook, “How to write with ChatGPT: without it sounding like ChatGPT”** (2025)
  [^cook-banlist]. Extensive (~200+ items across 10+ categories) banlist.
  Includes categories the others lack: “therapist mode,” “validation before dismissal,”
  “internal references,” and 10 contrast-reframe variations beyond the base “It’s not X,
  it’s Y.” template.
- **Hassid, “Ban”** (*How to AI* Substack, 2025)[^hassid-ban]. The only practitioner
  list whose rule formulations are derived from empirical frequency analysis (cross-LLM
  deep-research runs compared against arXiv corpus stats).
  Names patterns like “In a world where…” and the triple-negation setup as
  moralizing-generalization templates.
- **Orbach, “The ‘Anti-AI’ Writing Cheat Sheet”** (*Marketing Ideas* Substack,
  2026)[^orbach-anti-ai-cheat-sheet]. Every pattern in the 13-core-pattern, 24-rule set
  is paired with concrete before/after rewrite examples; closest thing in this corpus to
  a worked-example training set.
  Ships a ready-to-paste meta-prompt that bakes the rule set into an LLM session.
- **ai-boost, *awesome-prompts*: “29 signs of AI-generated text editor”** (2024–2025)
  [^ai-boost-awesome-prompts]. A 29-tell registry packaged as a prompt asset; uniquely
  includes “inflated symbolism” and “vague attributions” as distinct named tells
  alongside vocabulary and passive-voice categories.

**Negative finding:** as of mid-2026, no dedicated Vale style pack targets LLM register
specifically. Vale supports it through `existence` and `substitution` rules (see the
*Tools* section below), but no community pack exists yet.

## Tools for AI-Prose Detection and Linting

Open-source tools that help diagnose or remove AI register from drafts.
None ships a rule pack explicitly targeting LLM tells (*delve*, *Furthermore*,
self-negating parallels); the *Critique of AI-Authored Prose* section above lists the
practitioner rule sheets that would feed such a pack.

**AI-detection tools** (statistical, not rule-based).

- **GLTR (Giant Language model Test Room)**[^gltr]. Visualizes per-token likelihood
  under GPT-2 to surface the distributional fingerprints of machine-generated text.
  Apache 2.0; the early reference work (2019).
- **Binoculars**[^binoculars]. Zero-shot LLM-text detector that scores a passage by
  comparing perplexity across two closely related base models (Hans et al., ICML 2024).
  BSD-3-Clause.
- **Ghostbuster**[^ghostbuster]. Trains a classifier on token-probability features
  passed through weaker LMs to detect AI-generated text (Verma et al., 2023).

**Prose linters** (rule-based; AI-tell coverage incomplete).

- **proselint**[^proselint]. Python linter implementing rules drawn from named style
  authorities (Strunk, Garner, Pinker, etc.). Has adjacent rules for hedges, cliches,
  jargon, and corporate-speak that catch some AI tells (e.g., *leverage*, *utilize*) but
  no module specifically targeting LLM register.
  BSD-3-Clause; actively maintained.
- **Vale**[^vale]. Markup-aware prose linter built around configurable style packages
  (Microsoft, Google, write-good ports, custom YAML). No official AI-prose style pack,
  but the `existence` and `substitution` rule types make a community AI-tell pack
  straightforward to ship.
  MIT.
- **write-good**[^write-good]. Small Node linter flagging passive voice, weasel words,
  wordy phrases, and adverbs.
  MIT.
- **alex**[^alex]. retext-based linter for insensitive or inconsiderate language; often
  cited as the reference retext/unified plugin for building custom prose rules.
  MIT.
- **LanguageTool**[^languagetool]. Multilingual grammar, style, and spell checker with a
  large XML-rule engine and community style ruleset.
  Adjacent rules for redundancy, cliches, and register; no rules specifically calling
  out LLM-flavored prose.
  LGPL-2.1.
